I work close to glaciers in the Alps: Geoscience for All (Blog 1)

May 19, 2026

2026   ·   blog  

If I were to begin with no consideration for my readers, and in the way many (if not all) geoscience articles generally read, I would write something like the following as an introduction to my research.

For my PhD project, I am working in an alpine catchment called the Sulden catchment, which is located in the South Tyrol region of northern Italy. In my ongoing research, I focus on the proglacial part of this catchment. A widely accepted definition of a proglacial system is that it is the part of a glacierized catchment bounded by the Little Ice Age (LIA) terminal moraine and the contemporary glacier boundary. However, there have been a lot of modifications in the Sulden catchment due to human activities, and the LIA terminal moraine is no longer visible. Hence, I assume the proglacial region of this catchment to be limited by the terminal moraine of the 1927 glacier advance. During the 1920s, many glaciers in the Alps had witnessed advances.

That was enough jargon to begin with!

In my Geoscience for All blog series, I will try to write things in a way that allows everyone to understand the ideas with at least some clarity. I have struggled with geoscience jargon myself in the past. Now, with some experience, I can at least manage geomorphology papers (the study of the Earth’s changing surface and the sub-discipline I work in), but I still understand almost nothing when reading geochemistry papers, for example.

People in the field would argue that scientific papers are technical and that certain terms cannot be avoided. That is probably true, but here I will try to keep things as simple as possible so that anyone with a basic understanding of English and school-level science can follow along, even without a background in geoscience. In some posts, like this one, I will also explain some technical terms that can make other geoscience write-ups easier to understand.

For my uninitiated readers, this blog will cover a few glacial and near-glacial landscape terms (basically the terms in bold in my gibberish introductory paragraph) to help introduce you to my study region. I tend to avoid using AI for writing because I enjoy doing it myself. But I had a few conversations with ChatGPT to generate some instructive sketches explaining some basic concepts, mostly because I suck at drawing/sketching!

Catchment?

You can think of a catchment as a natural water-collecting system. Like a land, usually surrounded by mountains or hills, where rainfall flows downhill into streams and rivers that eventually drain through a single outlet.

AI helped me generate a nice sketch of a catchment :)

Catchment is quite a smart term, in my opinion. Think of individual pieces of land in terms of how much water they can “catch!”

Two catchments next to each other are separated by a catchment divide, which is a relatively higher-elevation ridge that separates rain falling between them.

Thinking of catchments within catchments (a nested approach) is also very interesting. What you call a catchment might contain a number of smaller sub-catchments within it, and those sub-catchments might contain even smaller sub-sub-catchments within them. At the same time, our original catchment could itself be part of a much bigger catchment.

For example, every small contributor of water to the river Ganga is part of the larger Ganga catchment. So, a tiny piece of land contributing water to the Ganga, located high up in the remote Himalaya, is also part of that bigger catchment. But while being part of the whole, if you focus only on that tiny bit of land, you could call it a catchment by itself too.

Little Ice Age (LIA)?

Despite its dramatic name, the LIA was not a true ice age, but a period of regional cooling that lasted roughly from the 13th to the 19th century. Many parts of the world became noticeably cooler, leading to the expansion and advance of glaciers in several mountain regions.

Moraine?

Imagine a mound of unsorted rocks and debris - that is what a moraine looks like. If you ask me, they are one of my favourite landforms to look at. They look fantastic in the field, and just as impressive in aerial photographs. Moraines are deposited by glaciers or ice sheets, which transport and leave behind all this material as they move and retreat.

In my opening paragraph, I had used the term terminal moraine. Moraines can be classified into different types:

  • A terminal moraine marks the terminus, or end, of an advancing glacier.
  • A medial moraine forms where two glacier ice streams merge.
  • A lateral moraine forms along the sides of a glacier.

Once we understand the LIA and terminal moraines, it becomes easier to imagine a proglacial area. It is simply the area between the current glacier boundary and the terminal moraine of the LIA glacier advance.

Now, it probably makes more sense to look at actual field photographs of different moraine types. Along with the moraines, you can also see some peaks and the proglacial stream flowing between moraines in this region.

I hope these explanations clarify some key glacial and proglacial terms. Let’s now look at the Sulden proglacial region itself. Here’s a Google Earth view of part of the catchment:

We have high peaks in the uplands, with elevations exceeding 3500 meters. You can also see the terminal moraine, which marks the former glacial extent in 1927.

Since then, the glacier has steadily receded, and this recession has become much more pronounced in recent years.

The LIA terminal moraine likely extended farther down-valley, but it is no longer preserved due to later human modifications of the landscape.
